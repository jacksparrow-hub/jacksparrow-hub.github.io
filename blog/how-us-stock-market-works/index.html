How the US Stock Market Works: Dow, S&P 500 and Nasdaq Explained

Every evening the news says something like "the Dow fell 200 points" or "the S&P 500 hit a record." If those lines sound like a secret code, this guide is for you. We'll keep it simple and stick to facts from official sources.

Disclaimer: This article is for education only. It is not investment advice, and we don't recommend buying or selling any stock.

What is a stock?

A stock (also called a share) is a small piece of ownership in a company. When a company "goes public," it sells shares that anyone can buy and sell on a stock exchange. If the company grows and earns more, people are usually willing to pay more for its shares. If it struggles, the price often drops.

Where are stocks traded?

The two best-known US exchanges are the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) and the Nasdaq. Buyers and sellers meet there through brokers, and prices change second by second based on supply and demand.

When is the US stock market open?

The NYSE core trading session runs from 9:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. Eastern Time, Monday to Friday, except market holidays (NYSE trading information). The exchange also publishes its full list of holidays for each year (NYSE hours and calendars).

Some trading also happens before and after these hours, but most of the day's activity, and the closing prices you see in the news, come from the core session.

Tip for readers outside the US: 9:30 a.m. ET is 2:30 p.m. in London for most of the year, and 7:00 p.m. in India during US daylight saving time (8:00 p.m. in winter).

What is a market index?

Nobody can watch thousands of stocks at once. So we use indices (or indexes). The US investor education site of the SEC explains that a market index "tracks the performance of a specific basket of stocks" that represents a market or sector (Investor.gov: Market Indices).

Think of an index as a thermometer. It doesn't tell you about every single stock, but it tells you whether the overall market is "warm" or "cold" today.

The Dow Jones Industrial Average (the Dow)

The Dow is an index of 30 large "blue chip" US company stocks (Investor.gov). Because it has only 30 companies, it's a narrow view of the market, but it's the oldest and most quoted index on TV.

Why "points" can mislead: A 300-point drop sounds scary, but when the Dow is at a high level, 300 points may be less than 1%. Always look at the percentage change, not just the points.

The S&P 500

As the name suggests, the S&P 500 follows about 500 large US companies. Because it's much broader than the Dow, many professionals treat it as the best single measure of the large-company US market. It is also the index that many index funds try to copy (Investor.gov: Index Funds).

The Nasdaq Composite

The Nasdaq Composite includes the stocks listed on the Nasdaq exchange. That exchange is home to many technology companies, so this index often moves more sharply when tech stocks rise or fall.

What is an index fund?

You can't buy an index directly, but you can buy a fund that follows one. Investor.gov describes an index fund as a mutual fund or ETF that "seeks to track the returns of a market index" (Investor.gov: Index Funds). Instead of picking stocks, the fund simply holds the stocks in the index.

People like index funds because they are simple and usually have low fees. But like any investment, their value goes up and down, and you can lose money.

Why do markets go up and down?

Prices move when the balance between buyers and sellers changes. Common reasons include:

  • Company results: Earnings reports that beat or miss expectations.
  • Interest rates: Changes or hints from the US Federal Reserve.
  • Economic data: Jobs reports, inflation numbers and GDP.
  • World events: Elections, conflicts, trade news.

Key terms in one minute

Term Meaning
Bull market A long period of rising prices
Bear market A long period of falling prices (often defined as a 20% drop from a high)
Ticker Short code for a stock, like a nickname
Volatility How much prices swing up and down
Dividend Part of a company's profit paid to shareholders

Summary

  • A stock is a piece of a company, traded on exchanges like the NYSE and Nasdaq.
  • The core US session is 9:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. ET on trading days.
  • The Dow (30 stocks), S&P 500 (about 500 stocks) and Nasdaq Composite are the main "thermometers."
  • Look at percentage moves, not just points.
