USB-C Cable Buying Checklist: Charging, Data Speed and Video

Short answer: A USB-C connector tells you its shape, not whether the cable can charge a laptop at its maximum rate, move large files quickly, or drive a monitor. Read three separate specifications: power rating in watts (W), data speed in gigabits per second (Gbps) or megabits per second (Mbps), and explicit display support. Then check the ports, charger, and display too. A cable cannot add display-output capability that the source device does not support.

Why the same USB-C shape can behave differently

USB Type-C is the physical connector. Charging power, data protocols, and display modes are separate capabilities. The PCWorld cable tests illustrate this: a particular 240W cable can still be USB 2.0 for data and may not support video, while a different tested cable can run a monitor. Power rating alone does not establish either capability. That article measured particular retail cables. We have not tested those cables or independently verified their current models and prices, so use its results only for the models and versions it actually tested.

Check 1: how much power must pass through?

Find your device's power requirement and your charger's advertised USB Power Delivery output, then choose a cable with a suitable published power rating. The USB-IF explains that USB Power Delivery 3.1 extended the system's potential power ceiling to 240W, with higher voltage levels. The word “potential” matters: a 240W-rated cable does not make a 65W charger provide 240W, and a device still negotiates what it accepts. A high-power cable rating alone says nothing reliable about fast file transfer or video.

Check 2: what data speed do you actually need?

For charging a phone and occasional small transfers, a cable specified at USB 2.0's 480 Mbps may be enough. For large video files or a fast external SSD, look for a documented 10, 20, 40, or 80 Gbps class that your devices also support. These are interface ceilings, not a promised real-world copy rate. Do not confuse Mbps with MB/s: one byte is eight bits before protocol overhead. Apple's cable comparison says its USB-C Charge Cable is limited to 480 Mbps data and does not support video, even though it charges devices. That is a concrete warning against treating “USB-C charging cable” as shorthand for high-speed data.

Check 3: is the whole display path supported?

If you intend to connect a laptop to a USB-C monitor or dock, check both device ports' display output/input capabilities and the cable maker's explicit video or compatible Thunderbolt/USB4 claim. A data-speed number alone is not a complete promise that your particular monitor resolution and refresh rate will work. Apple's Mac cable guide distinguishes charging and display use by accessory, while its Thunderbolt Pro cable guide describes display use for supported devices. Check the exact laptop, display, dock and cable specifications together, including any adapter in the chain.

How to read labels without overbuying

The USB-IF publishes certified cable logo guidelines that separate power and data markings. A genuine USB-IF Certified mark can help you read a package, but verify the exact model against the USB-IF Integrators List rather than trusting a marketplace photo alone. Do not assume every unmarked cable is defective. Look for the maker's exact model page and full specifications rather than an unqualified “fast” or “premium” headline. Keep a basic charging cable for travel if that is all you need; buy a higher-capability cable only for the device path you can name. Avoid damaged or overheating cables, and stop using one that becomes unusually hot.

Quick questions

Does 240W mean fast data and video?

No. Watts describe a cable's power rating, not its data wiring or display support. Apple's charge-cable example and PCWorld's tested models show why those must be checked separately.

Will an 80 Gbps cable make a 5 Gbps port faster?

No. The working connection depends on both endpoint ports and the cable; a higher cable rating cannot upgrade the device ports.

Can any USB-C phone connect directly to a monitor?

No. Check the phone's documented display-output feature and the display's input and cable requirements. Connector shape alone is not enough.
