Credit Report vs. Credit Score: A Plain-English US Guide

A credit report and a credit score are related, but they are not the same document. If you are trying to understand a lending decision, it helps to know which one you are looking at. This guide describes the US system; credit reporting rules and agencies differ elsewhere.

The difference in one minute

A credit report is a record of information about your credit activity and current accounts, such as payment history and account status. A credit score is a number calculated from information in a credit report. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) says there is no single universal score: the number can differ by reporting agency, scoring model, loan type and even the day it was calculated. (CFPB: credit report vs. credit score)

If you want to know... Look at...
Which accounts, balances and payment records are being reported? The credit report
What numerical estimate one particular model generated from report data? The credit score
Whether an account or payment is recorded incorrectly? The report first, then the relevant dispute process

A score cannot tell you which underlying entry may be wrong. Likewise, a credit report usually does not include a score. That is why getting a free report is not necessarily the same as getting a free score. (USAGov: credit reports)

Why can two scores be different?

A person can have many credit scores. The CFPB identifies several reasons for variation: the credit reporting agency supplying the data, the scoring model, the type of credit product, and the date. If you see one number in a banking app and another in a lender's notice, that difference alone does not prove an error. Compare the model and report source before drawing a conclusion. (CFPB: credit report vs. credit score)

Where can I get a free US credit report?

AnnualCreditReport.com says free weekly online reports are available from the three nationwide credit reporting companies: Equifax, Experian and TransUnion. USAGov identifies that site as the federally authorized source for free reports from the three agencies. You can choose which agency's report to request; each may contain somewhat different reported information.

Do not assume a site promising a 'free credit score' is the same service as the authorized report site. Follow the official URL directly rather than an ad or an unfamiliar lookalike.

Will requesting my own report lower my score?

No. The CFPB says checking your own report is not an inquiry for new credit and has no effect on your score. That is different from a lender assessing an application for new credit. The report check is useful for seeing the information that reporting companies may share with lenders; it is not a promise that a future application will be approved.

What if an item is wrong?

First, identify the exact entry and which agency's report shows it. The CFPB's dispute guide says correcting an error generally means contacting both the credit reporting company and the company that supplied the information. Explain what is wrong and why, provide copies of supporting documents, and keep copies of what you sent. The guide lists official dispute routes for each agency and explains the next steps if the investigation does not fix the issue. A dispute addresses inaccurate information; it is not a way to erase accurate history.

A simple reading checklist

  • Confirm the report belongs to you and look for accounts you do not recognize.
  • Check whether account status and payment history match your records.
  • If you spot an error, use the reporting company's dispute process and keep a paper trail.
  • Treat any score shown elsewhere as one model's estimate, not a fixed number shared by every lender.
